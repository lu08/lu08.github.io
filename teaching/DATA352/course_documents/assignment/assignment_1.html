DATA 352/551: In-Class Assignment 1

What Can Simulation Teach Us?

Group activity

Work in groups of 3–4. Your group will submit one response. You may use your Homework 1 solutions, lecture notes, and a calculator. You do not need to write executable code unless your group wants to use it to investigate an idea.

Suggested time: 25–35 minutes
Submission: One concise response per group, with each group member’s name

Your goal is to discuss what a simulation is measuring, what can go wrong, and how to communicate evidence from simulated data.

Part 1: Exact probability versus simulation

Let \(X\) be the number of heads in three tosses of a fair coin.

  1. Use the Homework 1 solution to identify \(P(X=0),\ldots,P(X=3)\).

  2. Imagine simulating 20, 100, and 10,000 sets of three tosses. What would you expect to happen to the simulated proportions as the number of repetitions increases?

  3. A group reports the following results from 20 repetitions:

    Number of heads 0 1 2 3
    Simulated count 1 8 7 4

    Which features of these results are consistent with the theoretical pmf? What would you avoid concluding from only 20 repetitions?

Part 2: Design a fair comparison

Your group wants to compare the probability that three fair dice have a sum of 9 with the probability that they have a sum of 10.

  1. Write a clear definition of one simulation. Be precise about what is randomly generated.
  2. What should be recorded after each simulation?
  3. How would you estimate the two probabilities from the recorded results?
  4. One student proposes simulating until the first sum of 9 appears and then comparing the number of rolls with the number of rolls until the first sum of 10 appears. Discuss why this is less useful than recording both outcomes across the same fixed number of repetitions.

Part 3: The martingale and misleading summaries

In the roulette martingale, a player stops after winning at least 5 dollars or losing more than 100 dollars. Red occurs with probability \(18/38\).

Discuss the following claim:

“The strategy is safe because most simulated games end with a win of 5 dollars.”

As a group, respond to these questions:

  1. What outcome should be recorded for each completed game?
  2. What additional quantities would help us understand the risk besides the proportion of games that end in a win?
  3. How could a small number of large losses affect the average net winnings, the median net winnings, and the proportion of winning games differently?
  4. Write a two- or three-sentence conclusion that would be more informative than the claim above.

Part 4: Why do smaller hospitals vary more?

In one town, a large hospital has about 45 births per day and a small hospital has about 15 births per day. The probability of a boy is 0.5 at both hospitals.

  1. Design a simulation that compares the number of days in one year on which more than 60% of births are boys at each hospital.
  2. Why can the small hospital have more days above 60% even though the underlying probability of a boy is the same?
  3. Predict what will happen to the difference between the hospitals if the daily birth counts become 90 and 30 instead of 45 and 15.
  4. State one lesson from this example about interpreting percentages from small samples.

Group submission checklist

Submit one response containing:

  • Your answers to Parts 1–4
  • At least one clearly written simulation design
  • One statement distinguishing a theoretical probability from a simulated estimate
  • One statement about variability or sample size
  • The names of all group members
