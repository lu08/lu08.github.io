Why transform a variable?

There are several common reasons to transform data:

  • convert from one unit to another, such as inches to centimeters or Celsius to Fahrenheit;
  • reverse the direction of a variable coding, such as changing (1 = Agree and 5 = Disagree) to (1 = Disagree and 5 = Agree);
  • standardize data so scores measured on different scales or unit can be compared.

The central question is: How does a transformation affect the distribution of a variable?

Specifically, we want to consider what happens to:

  • location: mode, median, and mean
  • spread: range, IQR, and standard deviation
  • shape: skewness ratio

Linear transformations

A linear transformation has the form

\[ \text{new data} = K*(\text{old data}) + C, \]

where K and C are constants.

  • K represents a scale factor
  • C represents a location shift

Examples:

  • new = 2(old) + 3 (scale everything up by 2; shift everything to the right by 3)

  • new = -1.5(old) - 10 (scale factor = -1.5; shift everything to the left by 10)

  • new = (old)/10 (scale factor = 1/10, no location shift)

Effect on location

Location statistics mimic the transformation:

\[ \text{new statistic} = K*(\text{old statistic}) + C \]

This rule applies to the mode, median, and mean.

For example, if the original mean is 20 and new = 3*(old) + 4, then

\[ \text{new mean} = 3*(20) + 4 = 64. \]

If the original median is 15 and new = -2*(old) + 5, then \[ \text{new median} = -2*(15) + 5 = -25. \]

Effect on spread

Spread statistics are affected by the magnitude of K, but not by C:

\[ \text{new spread} = |K|*(\text{old spread}) \]

This rule applies to the range, IQR, and standard deviation.

Note 1: These statistics are always positive, hence the absolute value in the formula.

Intution: the spread of a distribution is a measure of distance, which is always positive: the distance from my house to Drew is 2 miles, and the distance from Drew to my house is also 2 miles. You would never say that the distance from my house to Drew is “-2 miles.”

Note 2: The location shift C does not affect the spread of the distribution.

Intuition: if you move the entire distribution to the right or left, the distance between the values does not change (neither does the shape, so C also does not affect the skewness ratio).

For example, if the original data has a min of 0 and max of 4, then the range is 4. If new = 2*(old) + 3, then the new min is 3 and the new max is 11, so the new range is 8, or simply |2|(4) = 8. If new = -3*(old) + 4, then the new min is 4 and the new max is -8, so the new range is 12, or simply |-3|(4) = 12.

If the original standard deviation is 5 and new = -3*(old) + 4, then

\[ \text{new SD} = |-3|(5) = 15. \]

Effect on skewness

The skewness ratio is affected only by the sign of K:

\[ \text{new skewness ratio} = \begin{cases} \text{old skewness ratio}, & K > 0 \\ -\text{old skewness ratio}, & K < 0 \end{cases} \]

In other words, multiplying by a positive number does not change the skewness ratio. Multiplying by a negative number reverses the sign of the skewness ratio.

Intuition: imagine a distribution with a range of 0 to 10. If you multiply by 2, the range becomes 0 to 20, but the shape is the same. If you multiply by -2, the range becomes -20 to 0. The old minimum becomes the new maximum, and the shape is reversed: left skewed becomes right skewed, and right skewed becomes left skewed.

Example 1: Celsius to Fahrenheit

The conversion from Celsius to Fahrenheit is

\[ F = \frac{9}{5}C + 32. \]

Suppose a set of Celsius temperatures has mean 14.6, standard deviation 5, IQR 7, range 20, and skewness ratio 0.8. Calculate the corresponding Fahrenheit statistics.

Statistic Celsius Fahrenheit
Mean 14.6
Standard deviation 5
IQR 7
Range 20
Skewness ratio 0.8

In addition, as a review, what does the skewness ratio tell us about the shape of the distribution?

Show solution
Statistic Celsius Fahrenheit
Mean 14.6 58.28
Standard deviation 5 9
IQR 7 12.6
Range 20 36
Skewness ratio 0.8 0.8
The Fahrenheit distribution has the same shape, but a different center and spread because K = 9/5 is positive. The skewness ratio of 0.8 indicates that the distribution is roughly symmetric.

Example 2: reversing a scale

\[ \text{new data} = -2*(\text{old data}) + 5. \]

If the old mean is 3, the old standard deviation is 1.5, the old IQR is 2, and the old skewness ratio is 2.4, calculate the new statistics.

Statistic Old data New data
Mean 3
Standard deviation 1.5
IQR 2
Skewness ratio 2.4

In addition, what does the skewness ratio tell us about the shape of the distribution?

Show solution
Statistic Old data New data
Mean 3 -2(3) + 5 = -1
Standard deviation 1.5 |-2|(1.5) = 3
IQR 2 |-2|(2) = 4
Skewness ratio 2.4 -2.4
The negative value of K reverses the direction of the distribution. The skewness ratio of the old distribution, 2.4, indicates that the distribution is severely right-skewed. The new skewness ratio of -2.4 indicates that the distribution is severely left-skewed. The shape is reversed because we multiplied by a negative number.

Example 4: reverse a Likert scale

Suppose a Likert scale variable measures agreement with the statement “I like statistics”:

  • 1 = Strongly Agree
  • 2 = Agree
  • 3 = Neither agree nor disagree
  • 4 = Disagree
  • 5 = Strongly Disagree

Its summary statistics are mean = 2.56, median = 2, IQR = 2, and skewness ratio = 2.11.

We want to recode the variable so that high scores correspond to more interest in statistics:

  • 1 = Strongly Disagree
  • 2 = Disagree
  • 3 = Neither agree nor disagree
  • 4 = Agree
  • 5 = Strongly Agree
