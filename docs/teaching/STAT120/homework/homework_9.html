2. Numeric and numeric: steps

Consider the relationship between StepsBefore and StepsDuring.

  1. Use cor() with its default settings. Explain the result.

  2. Calculate the Pearson correlation using only complete pairs. Describe the direction and strength of the linear association in context. Do not use causal language.

  3. Use cor.test() on complete pairs. Report the p-value and state whether the correlation is statistically significant at the 0.05 level.

  4. Make a scatterplot of the two variables using either base R or ggplot2. Label both axes.

Show solution 
cor(COVIDCollege$StepsBefore, COVIDCollege$StepsDuring)

steps_complete <- COVIDCollege %>%
  drop_na(StepsBefore, StepsDuring)

cor(steps_complete$StepsBefore, steps_complete$StepsDuring)
cor.test(steps_complete$StepsBefore, steps_complete$StepsDuring)

plot(COVIDCollege$StepsBefore, COVIDCollege$StepsDuring,
     xlab = "Steps before COVID-19",
     ylab = "Steps during COVID-19",
     pch = 16,
     col = "salmon")

The default correlation is NA because of missing values. Using the 154 complete pairs, the correlation is about 0.531. This is a moderately positive linear association: students with more steps before COVID-19 tended to have more steps during COVID-19. The correlation test gives a p-value of approximately \(1.35 \times 10^{-12}\), which is below 0.05. This is evidence of a nonzero linear association in the population represented by the sampling process; it does not show that one measurement caused the other.

3. Numeric and categorical: sleep by sex

Investigate whether SleepDuring differs between the F and M groups in Sex.

  1. Inspect the values of Sex. For this comparison, retain only observations labeled F or M and report how many observations are in each group.

  2. Calculate the mean and median of SleepDuring for each group. Make a boxplot comparing the groups.

  3. Use t.test() to compare the mean sleep values. Report the p-value and write a conclusion in context. Avoid interpreting a nonsignificant result as proof that the groups are identical.

Show solution 
unique(COVIDCollege$Sex)

sleep_by_sex <- COVIDCollege %>%
  filter(Sex %in% c("F", "M")) %>%
  drop_na(SleepDuring)

sleep_by_sex %>%
  group_by(Sex) %>%
  summarise(
    n = n(),
    mean_sleep = mean(SleepDuring),
    median_sleep = median(SleepDuring)
  )

boxplot(SleepDuring ~ Sex,
        data = sleep_by_sex,
        xlab = "Sex",
        ylab = "Sleep during COVID-19",
        col = "salmon")

t.test(SleepDuring ~ Sex, data = sleep_by_sex)

There are 129 observations labeled F and 57 labeled M. The mean sleep values are about 7.72 hours for F and 7.89 hours for M. The Welch two-sample t-test gives a p-value of about 0.683. At the 0.05 level, the data do not provide statistically significant evidence of a difference in mean sleep between these groups. This does not prove that the means are identical.

4. Categorical and categorical: sex and sleep change

Create a categorical variable called SleepChange with two values:

  • Less sleep if SleepDuring is less than SleepBefore
  • Same or more sleep otherwise

Use only observations labeled F or M with complete values for both sleep variables.

  1. Make a contingency table of Sex and SleepChange.

  2. Calculate row proportions. Compare the proportion with less sleep in the two groups.

  3. Use a chi-square test to assess whether Sex and SleepChange are associated. Report the p-value and state a conclusion.

Show solution 
sleep_change <- COVIDCollege %>%
  filter(Sex %in% c("F", "M")) %>%
  drop_na(SleepBefore, SleepDuring) %>%
  mutate(SleepChange = if_else(
    SleepDuring < SleepBefore,
    "Less sleep",
    "Same or more sleep"
  ))

sleep_table <- table(sleep_change$Sex, sleep_change$SleepChange)
sleep_table
prop.table(sleep_table, margin = 1)
chisq.test(sleep_table)

The counts are 46 F and 23 M observations with less sleep. The row proportions are about 35.7% for F and 40.4% for M. The chi-square test gives a p-value of about 0.656. At the 0.05 level, there is not statistically significant evidence of an association between Sex and this two-category sleep-change variable.
